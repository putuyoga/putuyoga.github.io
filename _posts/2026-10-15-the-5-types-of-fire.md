Imagine two people in Indonesia, both 35 years old, with Rp3 billion (approximately USD 170,000) in investments.

The first lives in Salatiga, a small town in Central Java, owns a modest house, has no debt, and spends around Rp7 million per month. The second lives in Jakarta, rents an apartment, has two children, and spends Rp25 million monthly.

At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, Rp3 billion could initially support about Rp8.75 million per month. The first person has already exceeded their theoretical FIRE target of Rp2.4 billion. The second would need approximately Rp8.57 billion to maintain their lifestyle without employment.

This post continues some of the ideas I explored in [Compounding Your Life Force](/posts/compounding-your-life-force) and [The Fall of the Tech Gold Rush](/posts/the-fall-of-the-tech-gold-rush).

I've been pursuing financial independence for years, but the desire became much stronger during COVID. That period made me realize how fragile employment could be, even at companies we considered stable. Since then, I've become increasingly defensive about my financial security.

That's what first drew me to **Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE)** movement. The main point is how much you need to sustain your lifestyle.

FIRE comes in several variations: **Lean FIRE**, **Regular FIRE**, **Fat FIRE**, **Barista FIRE**, and **Coast FIRE**. There are more variations out there, but I think these five explain the basic concept well enough.

## Understanding the FIRE Number

The most popular starting point is the **4% rule**. It suggests withdrawing 4% of your investment portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjusting that amount for inflation in subsequent years. The original research focused primarily on retirement periods of around 30 years.

Suppose your household spends Rp10 million per month, or Rp120 million annually. Using the 4% rule, you'd need approximately Rp3 billion invested.

**FIRE Number = Annual Expenses / Withdrawal Rate**

However retiring at 35 is different from retiring at 60. Your investments might have to support you for another 50 years or more, so I prefer using a more conservative 3–3.5% withdrawal rate when thinking about early retirement. At 3.5%, the same lifestyle would require roughly Rp3.43 billion.

One important caveat: we're talking about *investable assets*, not just total net worth. Your house, car, motorcycle, or even your PS5 might be worth something, but they won't pay for groceries unless you sell them or generate income from them. A home you live in still has value, of course; it also saves you from paying rent.

A withdrawal rate isn't a guaranteed investment return, either. Markets fluctuate, inflation changes purchasing power, and taxes and unexpected expenses complicate the calculation. These are planning guidelines, not promises.

I also found this [FIRE planner](https://firenum.com/fire-planner) useful if you'd like to run your own numbers.

Now, let's look at five fictional people whose situations illustrate what FIRE might actually look like in Indonesia.

## 1. Lean FIRE: Living Modestly in a Small City

<img src="/assets/images/fire-01.jpg" />

Meet Rina, a 38-year-old former administrative employee living in Malang. She owns a modest house, has no mortgage, and doesn't have children.

Her monthly expenses look something like this:

| Expense | Monthly |
|---|---:|
| Groceries | Rp2.2M |
| Utilities and internet | Rp800K |
| Transportation | Rp700K |
| Healthcare and insurance | Rp700K |
| Family support | Rp1M |
| Entertainment | Rp600K |
| Irregular expenses reserve | Rp1M |
| **Total** | **Rp7M** |

Rina has accumulated Rp2.4 billion in diversified investments. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, her portfolio could initially support Rp84 million annually, equivalent to Rp7 million per month. In theory, she's reached **Lean FIRE**.

She no longer has to wake up early, commute to the office, or attend meetings about why the previous meeting didn't produce enough progress. Instead, she gardens, reads, cooks, and occasionally visits friends.

The catch is that her financial margin is thin. A major medical expense, house repair, or prolonged market downturn could disrupt her plan. She'll need an emergency reserve beyond ordinary spending and the flexibility to cut expenses when necessary.

Lean FIRE works best when modest living is something you genuinely enjoy, not a punishment you endure to escape employment. Location matters, too. Rp7 million in Malang buys a different lifestyle from Rp7 million in central Jakarta.

## 2. Regular FIRE: Maintaining a Comfortable Lifestyle

<img src="/assets/images/fire-02.jpg" />

Now consider Andi and Maya, a married couple in their early 40s living in Jakarta. They don't have children, but they rent an apartment, own a car, enjoy dining out, and take occasional vacations.

| Expense | Monthly |
|---|---:|
| Apartment rental | Rp5M |
| Groceries and dining | Rp4M |
| Transportation | Rp2M |
| Utilities and internet | Rp1.5M |
| Healthcare and insurance | Rp1.5M |
| Entertainment | Rp1M |
| Travel and maintenance reserve | Rp1M |
| **Total** | **Rp16M** |

At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, they'd need approximately **Rp5.49 billion invested**. Suppose they've accumulated Rp5.5 billion in diversified financial assets. They could theoretically stop working without dramatically changing their lifestyle.

They can continue renting, enjoy their favorite restaurants, and travel occasionally. They're not necessarily rich enough to buy everything they want, but they're no longer dependent on a monthly paycheck. They would still need to watch rising rent, healthcare costs, and market downturns, and adjust their spending if necessary.

That's **Regular FIRE** to me: enough investments to maintain a lifestyle you're already comfortable with. Not necessarily rich enough to fly business class everywhere, but secure enough that losing a job is no longer a financial emergency.

## 3. Fat FIRE: Retiring Without Giving Up Luxury

<img src="/assets/images/fire-03.jpg" />

Imagine a successful business owner in Jakarta who enjoys frequent international vacations, expensive restaurants, premium cars, and luxurious accommodation.

Their household spends Rp35 million per month, or Rp420 million annually. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, they'd need approximately **Rp12 billion invested** to sustain that lifestyle without employment.

That's the idea behind **Fat FIRE**: retiring while maintaining relatively expensive habits. There's no universal monetary threshold separating Lean, Regular, and Fat FIRE. What counts as luxurious in a small Indonesian city might be ordinary in a more expensive place.

To me, the more interesting question is the trade-off. Imagine someone who could retire comfortably at 40 with Rp5 billion but decides to work another ten years to accumulate Rp12 billion.

Maybe they genuinely enjoy their career, have expensive hobbies, or want more security for their family. Fair enough. But maybe they're just moving the goalposts every time they get close.

At some point, is another luxury car or slightly nicer hotel worth several more years of your life? Imagine working excessively hard to achieve Fat FIRE, only to discover that your favorite activities are reading books, playing video games, and jogging around the neighborhood.

That would be a rather expensive realization, wouldn't it?

## 4. Barista FIRE: The Software Engineer Who Leaves Corporate Life

<img src="/assets/images/fire-04.jpg" />

This is where FIRE gets particularly interesting to me.

Meet Budi, a 34-year-old software engineer living in Yogyakarta. He's worked in tech for more than a decade, earned a good salary, and consistently invested a large portion of it.

After years of sprint planning, production incidents, unreasonable deadlines, and meetings that could've been a three-line Slack message, he starts wondering whether he really wants another twenty years of the same thing.

Fortunately, Budi has accumulated Rp2.8 billion in financial assets. His household spends Rp13 million per month. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, his portfolio could initially support about Rp8.17 million monthly. Full FIRE at his spending level would require approximately Rp4.46 billion.

But here's the interesting part: **Budi doesn't necessarily need another high-paying corporate job.**

He leaves full-time employment and takes occasional freelance software projects, earning Rp7 million monthly after taxes and business expenses while working fewer hours.

| Item | Monthly |
|---|---:|
| Living expenses | Rp13M |
| Freelance income | Rp7M |
| Required portfolio withdrawal | Rp6M |

Withdrawing Rp6 million per month from Rp2.8 billion represents an initial annual withdrawal rate of roughly **2.57%**.

Suddenly, the equation changes. Budi can choose clients, control his schedule, and spend more time on work he actually enjoys. Perhaps he works three days a week, builds a small SaaS product, or occasionally helps companies solve difficult technical problems.

Freelancing isn't guaranteed income, of course. Clients disappear, projects get delayed, and sometimes the market decides your brilliant software product is worth precisely nothing. He still needs cash reserves and a fallback plan.

But that's **Barista FIRE**: investments cover part of your living expenses, while less demanding work covers the rest. And no, you don't literally have to become a barista, although making latte art might occasionally sound more satisfying than debugging a mysterious production incident on Friday evening.

What I like about this approach is the distinction between enjoying your profession and needing to maximize income. Someone who genuinely loves programming might simply want to escape the corporate machinery surrounding it, not abandon software engineering altogether.

## 5. Coast FIRE: Let Compound Interest Do the Heavy Lifting

<img src="/assets/images/fire-05.jpg" />

Consider Nadia, a 30-year-old professional living in Surabaya. She started working in her early twenties, saved aggressively, and accumulated Rp1.5 billion in investments before turning 30.

Her goal is to retire at 55 with the equivalent of Rp12 million in monthly spending, measured in today's purchasing power. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, she'd need approximately Rp4.11 billion in today's money.

Suppose her investments grow by an average of **4.5% annually after inflation**. Over 25 years, her existing Rp1.5 billion could theoretically grow to around Rp4.51 billion without another contribution or any withdrawals.

In other words, Nadia might already have enough invested for her future retirement goal. She still needs employment income to cover her expenses today, but she may no longer need to save aggressively for retirement.

That gives her more career flexibility. She could take a lower-paying job with better hours, decline a promotion involving twenty direct reports and half a lifetime of meetings, or change careers entirely, as long as she can pay her current bills without touching the portfolio.

The assumed return isn't guaranteed. If markets disappoint, she may need to contribute more or retire later. But **Coast FIRE** offers a different perspective: you don't necessarily need full financial independence to stop organizing your career around earning as much as possible.

The math is relatively simple. The difficult part is convincing yourself that you don't have to chase every salary increase.

## Comparing the Five Types

| FIRE Type | Investments | Lifestyle |
|---|---:|---|
| Lean FIRE | Rp2.4B | Rp7M/month, modest lifestyle |
| Regular FIRE | Rp5.49B | Rp16M/month, comfortable lifestyle |
| Fat FIRE | Rp12B | Rp35M/month, luxurious lifestyle |
| Barista FIRE | Rp2.8B | Rp13M/month, supported by Rp7M earned income |
| Coast FIRE | Rp1.5B at age 30 | Work while investments compound until age 55 |

These five types really answer two questions. **Lean, Regular, and Fat FIRE** describe how much you need to sustain your preferred lifestyle. **Barista and Coast FIRE** describe how you can reduce your dependence on full-time employment before reaching complete financial independence.

They aren't mutually exclusive. Someone could reach Coast FIRE at 30, transition into Barista FIRE at 40, and eventually achieve Regular FIRE without returning to conventional full-time employment.

All these amounts are illustrative, not guarantees. Actual outcomes depend on investment performance, inflation, taxes, withdrawal timing, and spending flexibility.

## Not All Wealth Gives You Freedom

There's another part of financial independence that doesn't get enough attention: the difference between being wealthy on paper and having assets you can actually use.

Imagine someone with Rp8 billion in total assets: Rp3 billion in inherited land tied up in family disputes, Rp2 billion in a primary residence, Rp1 billion in private-company equity with no clear way to sell, and just Rp2 billion in accessible financial investments.

Can they confidently retire based on Rp8 billion? Not necessarily. At a 3.5% withdrawal rate, their Rp2 billion portfolio could initially support about Rp5.83 million per month. Their home saves them rent, but the other assets can't directly fund living expenses until they generate income or become liquid.

And there's a cost that rarely appears in spreadsheets: the baggage attached to certain assets. An inheritance might come with family disputes, expectations, obligations, or relationships you'd rather not maintain. Its financial value could be substantial, but the personal cost might be higher.

Likewise, private-company stock options might theoretically be worth hundreds of millions of rupiah, but until there's an actual opportunity to exercise and sell them, their paper valuation won't pay your electricity bill.

**Financial independence isn't just about accumulating assets. It's about accumulating assets that let you make decisions independently.** What's the point of buying freedom if acquiring more wealth forces you to surrender the freedom you've already built?

## Financial Independence Isn't Necessarily About Retirement

After exploring these approaches, I realized the most valuable part of FIRE isn't necessarily retiring early. It's being able to make decisions without money dictating every aspect of your life.

Imagine leaving an unhealthy workplace without worrying about next month's bills. Or taking eight months to learn something random, build an independent product, travel, or simply do nothing particularly productive.

You could still work full-time if you genuinely enjoy it. The difference is that you wouldn't have to tolerate an unhealthy environment just because you need the next paycheck.

## Knowing When Enough Is Enough

Perhaps the biggest challenge with FIRE isn't understanding the mathematics, but knowing when enough is actually enough.

Once you accumulate Rp3 billion, Rp4 billion starts looking attractive. Then Rp6 billion, and eventually Rp10 billion, even though your living expenses haven't changed much. There's always another number to chase, and working another year can always be justified financially.

But at what cost? Your health, relationships, or peace of mind? I'm not suggesting everyone should resign immediately after reaching their FIRE number. However, at some point, another hundred millions of rupiah might be worth less than having control over your own time.

Ultimately, **money is a tool for acquiring freedom, not the destination itself**. The greatest luxury might simply be waking up on a Monday morning, knowing you can decide what to do with your day and, more importantly, what you can say no to.

That, to me, is the whole point of FIRE. **Freedom.**
